---
title: "Accessibility"
description: "Accessible interfaces work for everyone. They support different abilities, contexts, and ways of interacting with technology. Good accessibility makes information and interactions available to all people."

---

## Vision

People may have low vision, color blindness, light sensitivity, or difficulty reading in certain environments. Support larger text sizes, allow people to resize content comfortably, and keep text easy to read in all conditions.

- Use clear type sizes and comfortable line lengths.
- Keep contrast high between text, icons, and backgrounds.
- Avoid relying on color alone to communicate meaning.
- Use labels, icons, shapes, and patterns to reinforce important information.
- Describe interface elements clearly for screen readers and other assistive technologies.
## Hearing

People may be deaf or hard of hearing, or may use devices in noisy environments. Do not communicate important information through sound alone.

- Provide captions, subtitles, transcripts, and other text alternatives.
- Pair audio cues with visual or tactile feedback.
- Add clear visual indicators when sound is used to guide action.
- Make media controls easy to find and use.
## Mobility

Design for people with limited dexterity or mobility. Controls should be easy to reach, activate, and understand.

- Make controls large enough and give them comfortable spacing.
- Reduce the need for complex gestures.
- Offer more than one way to complete common actions.
- Support keyboard-only navigation, voice input, and switch-based interaction where appropriate.
- Keep labels and targets predictable and easy to identify.
## Speech

Support people who use text-based, keyboard-only, or voice-based interactions. Keep the interface predictable and easy to navigate without a single input method.

- Allow people to navigate and complete actions without using a mouse or touch gestures.
- Provide clear labels and logical focus order.
- Support voice input and switch-based control when relevant.
- Keep commands understandable and consistent.
## Cognitive

Reduce complexity and help people understand what to do next.

- Use familiar patterns and consistent interactions.
- Keep actions clear and easy to remember.
- Avoid time-limited UI unless it is essential.
- Break complex flows into smaller, manageable steps.
- Ask for confirmation before important or destructive actions.
- Limit unnecessary motion and avoid flashing or distracting animation.
- Offer controls to pause, stop, replay, or adjust media when needed.
## General practices

- Provide clear labels for all controls and content.
- Maintain visible focus states and strong keyboard support.
- Respect reduced-motion and other accessibility preferences.
- Support personalization where it improves usability.
- Test with real assistive technologies and with people who use them.
